## Direction A — Weekly Beat / إيقاع الأسبوع

**Idea in one sentence:** The week has seven beats and one of them is your roast, so the whole identity is built on a seven-column grid with one column lit.

**Strategic reason:** The strategy says "reliability is the brand" and asks for "a consistent, repeatable system; dates and weights given real prominence". This direction turns the only verified differentiator (roasts to order weekly) into the structure of the identity itself, so the promise is visible before a word is read. It speaks first to the primary audience, the café owner, who is persuaded by specifics.

| Trait from the strategy | How A shows it |
|---|---|
| Dependable | One device, one grid, repeated identically every week |
| Rooted, Arabic-led | Arabic wordmark and headline lead; the bar reads right to left |
| Plain-spoken | Facts at headline size; weights and numbers large |
| Hospitable | Carried by the warm ember red and cream, and by wording. This is A's weakest trait |

**Colour direction**

| Name | HEX | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Ember — جمر | `#C23A20` | Lead colour; the lit bar; 1 kg bag |
| Ink — حبر | `#1F1B18` | Text; the six unlit bars |
| Paper — ورق | `#F3ECE0` | Background |
| Crema — كريما | `#E3C9A0` | Secondary surface |
| Ash — رماد | `#6F6861` | Captions and small technical text |

Contrast, calculated: Ink on Paper 14.6:1; Ember on Paper and Paper on Ember 4.6:1 (AA for body text, but with little margin); Ash on Paper 4.7:1; Ink on Crema 10.7:1. Ink on Ember is 3.9:1, large text only.

**Typography direction**

- Arabic: **IBM Plex Sans Arabic** — Bold for headlines, Medium for sub-heads, Regular for text.
- Latin: **IBM Plex Sans** — same weights; Light, slightly condensed, for large figures (250 g, 1 kg).
- Why: one family designed across both scripts, so stroke and tone match by construction. Its engineered, slightly technical character says "dependable" without looking corporate.
- Note: IBM Plex Sans in the repo is a variable font; print work needs static instances (`file-delivery.md`).

**Graphic and imagery language**

- The device: seven bars, one lit. Used large as a band, small as a rule under the wordmark, repeated in rows as the pattern.
- Layouts sit on seven columns. Hairline rules, flat colour, square corners.
- No illustration. If photography is added later: beans, bags and equipment only, shot flat and frontal on Paper or Crema. No people.

**Rough logo idea (in words):** The Arabic word سدرة drawn as a sturdy low-contrast wordmark, sitting on a row of seven short bars with one in Ember. "SIDRA COFFEE" in spaced Latin capitals beneath, aligned to the same width. The icon-only version is the seven bars alone in a square. The board shows a type-set sketch, not a drawn logo.

**Sample application on the board:** 1 kg wholesale bag in Ember and 250 g bag in Paper.

**Risks**

- Which bar is lit implies a roast day. The client has not confirmed a roast day, so the position is illustrative. If no day can ever be published, the lit bar becomes a neutral "one in seven" and loses some meaning.
- Seven vertical bars can read as a barcode or an equaliser at small sizes. The identity-designer must test proportions at 16 px and 10 mm.
- It is the cooler of the two. Hospitality rests on colour and copy alone.

**Distinct from the competitors (against unverified assumptions)**

- *Camel Step* (assumed: illustrated camel emblem, heritage, warm earthy palette): A has no emblem, no animal, no heritage reference. Its symbol is a schedule.
- *Elixir Bunn* (assumed: dark, minimal, Latin-prominent, premium): A is light-ground and red-led, with Arabic leading. It shows a delivery rhythm, not rarity.
- *Brew92* (assumed: clean typographic wordmark, neutral palette, English-led): this is the nearest neighbour, because A is also typographic. The difference is the Arabic-first wordmark, a saturated lead colour, and a device that carries a meaning. If inspection shows Brew92 uses a strong red, change Ember.

## Direction B — Nabq / نَبْق

**Idea in one sentence:** The sidr tree's fruit is round and warm like a coffee cherry, so one dot becomes the mark of hospitality on everything Sidra hands over.

**Strategic reason:** The strategy keeps hospitality as a core trait and says warmth must be "carried by colour, materials and wording, never by pictures of people". The audit's white space asks for "a natural, local symbol that is not the camel or the desert" and a tree used "through form, not in green and white". The fruit answers all three: a local root, a warm palette that comes from the subject, and a shape already native to Arabic script (the two dots of the ة in سدرة).

| Trait from the strategy | How B shows it |
|---|---|
| Dependable | A small fixed set of circles and colours. This is B's weakest trait |
| Rooted, Arabic-led | The symbol lives inside the Arabic word; it cannot be made from the Latin name |
| Plain-spoken | Flat shapes, short lines, no ornament |
| Hospitable | Round forms, ripe colours, generous scale |

**Colour direction**

| Name | HEX | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Shade — ظلّ | `#3B2A3F` | Lead dark; text; the tree's shade |
| Nabq — نبق | `#E2A12E` | Lead bright; the fruit; 1 kg bag |
| Clay — طين | `#A64E2C` | The ripe fruit; accents and sub-heads |
| Bark — لحاء | `#5A3A28` | Supporting dark; captions |
| Sand — رمل | `#F2E6D2` | Background |

Contrast, calculated: Shade on Sand 10.7:1; Shade on Nabq 5.9:1; Bark on Sand 8.2:1; Clay on Sand 4.5:1 (AA for body text, with almost no margin). Nabq on Sand fails for text and is used for shapes only.

**Typography direction**

- Arabic: **Tajawal** — ExtraBold for headlines, Medium for sub-heads, Regular for text.
- Latin: **Inter** — the same three weights.
- Why: Tajawal's low contrast and open, rounded counters echo the dot and feel warm at large sizes. Inter matches it in stroke contrast and plainness. They are not one family, so the identity-designer must match optical size (Arabic set larger) and weight by eye.
- Note: Inter in the repo is a variable font; print work needs static instances.

**Graphic and imagery language**

- The device: the two dots of the ة drawn as two fruits, one amber and one ripe.
- Shade: one large quiet circle with one bright dot beneath it. Pattern: rows of fruit with one ripe in each row, which is a quiet nod to "one roast each week".
- Flat circles, large radii on panels, overlapping colour. No botanical illustration, no leaves, no ornament.
- If photography is added later: beans and bags on Sand or Shade, in warm side light. No people.

**Rough logo idea (in words):** The Arabic word سدرة drawn as a soft, heavy wordmark in which the two dots of the ة are replaced by two round fruits, amber and clay. "Sidra Coffee" in Latin sentence case beneath. The icon-only version is the two fruits alone. The board shows the typeface with coloured circles laid over its dots, not a drawn logo.

**Sample application on the board:** 1 kg bag in Nabq amber and 250 g bag in Sand.

**Risks**

- It depends on the name's link to the sidr tree, which is open question 4 in the strategy and not confirmed by the client. If the client says no, B falls.
- The claim that the sidr fruit (nabq) is round, amber to red-brown and resembles a coffee cherry is my general knowledge and is not sourced. It needs checking before any copy says so.
- The strategy considered and did not choose a tree-led strategy ("Under the sidr") because it speaks mainly to home brewers. B is a visual direction for the approved strategy, not a return to that one, but it shares the weakness: it gives a wholesale buyer warmth, not proof.
- Coloured circles are a common device. The two-dots-in-the-word idea is what makes it ownable, so the logo must carry it.
- Camel Step is assumed to use a warm earthy palette. If confirmed, B sits closer to it in colour than A does.

**Distinct from the competitors (against unverified assumptions)**

- *Camel Step* (assumed: illustrated camel emblem, heritage, warm earthy palette): B is abstract geometry, not illustration, and plant not animal. It uses plum as its dark instead of brown or black. Palette overlap is the point to check.
- *Elixir Bunn* (assumed: dark, minimal, Latin-prominent, premium): B is colourful, round and Arabic-led. Its dark is a warm plum used as shade, not a black luxury ground.
- *Brew92* (assumed: clean typographic wordmark, neutral palette, English-led): B is symbol-led and saturated, and its symbol exists only in the Arabic spelling.

## How the two differ

| | A — Weekly Beat | B — Nabq |
|---|---|---|
| The idea | The schedule is the identity | The host's offering is the identity |
| Leads with | Dependable, plain-spoken | Hospitable, rooted |
| Speaks first to | Café owner (primary audience) | Home brewer (secondary audience) |
| Form | Square, gridded, seven columns | Round, overlapping, free |
| Symbol | Seven bars, one lit | Two dots of the ة as fruit |
| Rests on | A verified fact (roasts weekly) | An unconfirmed link (name and tree) |

## Recommendation: Direction A — Weekly Beat

1. **It is the approved strategy made visible.** "Your order, roasted this week" can be read from the mark itself. B needs the copy to say it.
2. **It serves the business goal.** The brief asks for 20 café accounts and credibility with wholesale buyers. A looks like a supplier with a system.
3. **It rests on a verified fact.** B rests on a link the client has not confirmed.
4. **It is the further of the two from the camel-and-heritage territory** and from any earthy palette.

What I would carry from B into A if the Creative Director agrees: its warmth. A's hospitality is thin, and Crema could be used more generously, or one rounder gesture introduced, when `creative-direction.md` is written. That is a refinement of A, not a blend of the two.
